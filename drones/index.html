How far is India from a 100% indigenous drone?

A drone is a stack of a dozen technologies: airframe, motors, battery, flight controller, navigation, radio link, camera, engine, autopilot software and AI. India now assembles thousands of drones and has fought with them, but the parts inside are often imported, mostly from China. This tracker scores each layer, names the companies and the money behind them, and lists what still blocks a drone that is Indian all the way down.

India must work in mission mode to “emerge as a global hub of indigenous drone manufacturing”.
— Rajnath Singh, Defence Minister, March 2026. The government's stated goal since 2021: a global drone hub by 2030.

Why industry says “70% indigenous”

The Drone Federation of India says the top 10% of makers, who hold about 80% of the market, are about 70% indigenous. Most smaller firms import more than half their parts. Those figures count work done in India, such as boards assembled and airframes moulded here, even when the chips, magnets and cells inside are imported. Our ~29% counts only value from Indian-owned design and production down to the part. Both are correct; they answer different questions.

What the rules actually require

Since February 2022 India bans imports of complete drones, but parts can be imported freely. Since November 2023, military drones must have at least 50% indigenous content by value, and Chinese sub-components are barred. No Indian body has been mandated to certify this, and in 2025 the Army cancelled orders for 400 logistics drones after Chinese parts were found inside.

Indian design, imported gutsThe best Indian drones own their airframe, autopilot software and ground station. Their chips, magnets, battery cells and thermal sensors are usually imported, which is why hardware scores sit around 40–60%.
“Made in India” can mean licence-builtDrishti-10 is built in Hyderabad and Adani says it has over 70% indigenous content, but the design, avionics and software are Elbit's Hermes 900. Indian assembly does not mean Indian control.
The big drones are the least IndianIndia's largest drones are Israeli Herons, licence-built Hermes and US MQ-9Bs on order. The indigenous MALE drone, Tapas-BH-201, still flies on an imported Austrian engine and has missed its altitude and endurance goals.

Ratings are our assessment from public reporting (company statements, DRDO, Business Standard, Swarajya, Outlook Business, The Week, Wikipedia). Most makers do not disclose their part suppliers; where a part isn't disclosed it is scored as foreign. Hardware weights follow the bill of materials above.

Not a conglomerate? Ways a startup, college or individual can help

Build open avionicsContribute NavIC support and Indian hardware targets to open autopilots (ArduPilot, PX4), and publish test results so buyers can trust Indian boards.
Make one part, wellMotors, ESCs, propellers, gimbals and connectors are small-capital businesses. One reliable Indian part beats one more assembled drone.
Become a certified pilot244 DGCA-approved schools train remote pilots. Agri spraying, survey and inspection need operators far more than new airframes.
Buy and specify IndianBuyers who ask for a parts declaration, as the Army now does, are what push makers to replace Chinese components.
04 · Investment

The money: government schemes and big orders

The civil drone incentive was tiny: ₹120 crore of PLI over three years. Defence demand is what changed the industry, especially after Operation Sindoor in May 2025. Most of the money buys finished drones; very little yet goes to the parts where the dependence is greatest. The component mission and PLI 2.0 are still proposals.

05c · Talent

Who builds it: where the people come from, and who is training the next wave

The 2021 Drone Rules created India's first large-scale drone workforce: certified remote pilots trained at DGCA-approved schools. Engineering talent came earlier, from DRDO's aeronautics labs, IIT aerospace departments and the hobbyist community. Since Operation Sindoor the Army has become the biggest trainer of all.

Leaders and their backgrounds

Students built the first Indian drone companyideaForge began as an IIT Bombay student project in the 2000s and grew into India's largest drone maker. Many component startups also come from campus and hobbyist teams.
Military know-how comes from veterans and DRDODRDO's Aeronautical Development Establishment has built drones since the 1980s, and ex-service officers lead several defence-drone startups, including NewSpace Research.
The pilots are ahead of the partsIndia trains tens of thousands of pilots, but very few engineers in motor design, RF, thermal sensors or battery chemistry. That is where the import gap is.

06 · History

Forty years of drones: 1985 → 2030

India started building drones in the 1980s, then bought Israeli ones for two decades. Civilian drones were effectively banned from 2014 to 2021. Since then, liberal rules, an import ban and war have built an industry, but not yet its supply chain.

08 · Roadmap

How far, realistically?

Our assessment of when each level of “indigenous” becomes realistic, given current projects, funding and global supply chains.

Level 1
Today–2027

Indian-designed, no Chinese parts

  • Indian airframe, autopilot software, ground station and assembly
  • Chinese parts replaced with Taiwanese, US, European or Indian ones
  • Indian motors and ESCs in tactical drones
  • Imported chips, magnets, cells and thermal sensors

~30–35% Indian-owned value. Largely achieved by the top makers.

Level 2
2027–2030

Indian propulsion, payloads and links

  • Indian rare-earth magnets in motors (magnet scheme, from ~2028)
  • Indian EO/IR payloads and gimbals in volume
  • NavIC navigation and EW-resistant Indian datalinks as standard
  • Indian 180 hp engine flying on a MALE drone

~45–50% Indian-owned value. Plausible if the component mission is approved.

Level 3
2030–35

Indian silicon and cells

  • Indian flight-control chip (RISC-V MCU or SoC), made at Tata Dholera
  • Indian drone-grade battery cells
  • Indian thermal sensor (microbolometer)
  • Indigenous MALE and loyal-wingman drones in service

~65–75% Indian-owned value. Needs the semiconductor and cell programmes to deliver.

Level 4
2035+

100% indigenous

  • Indian image sensors, IMU MEMS and RF chips
  • Indian turbofan engines for UCAVs
  • Full rare-earth mining-to-magnet chain

Few countries do this alone. A drone that is “Indian-controlled, no single-country dependence” is the realistic target.
